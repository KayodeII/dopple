# Nigeria's school system, nursery to SS3: what Dopple must serve

Research notes, October 2026. They shape the product: what levels and subjects exist, which exams
learners face, who is left out, and what limits them (money, data, power, phones). Sources are at
the end; figures from news and third-party sites are marked as such. **Check the official source
before putting any of this in front of learners as fact.**

## 1. Structure

| Stage | Classes | Typical age | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Early years (ECCDE) | Nursery / kindergarten, then **one pre-primary year** | 3–5 | The 2013 National Policy on Education made one pre-primary year part of basic education (the "1-6-3-3-4" system). Nursery and kindergarten before that are mostly private. Only ~45% of pupils in P1–JSS in 2015 had attended pre-school. |
| Primary | Primary 1–6 | 6–11 | Split into lower (P1–3) and upper (P4–6) for the curriculum. |
| Junior secondary (JSS) | JSS 1–3 | 12–14 | Ends with the BECE. Primary + JSS are the 9 years of free, compulsory basic education under the UBE Act 2004. |
| Senior secondary (SSS) | SS 1–3 | 15–17 | Ends with WASSCE / NECO SSCE (or NABTEB for technical). |

- **Free and compulsory:** the Compulsory, Free Universal Basic Education Act 2004 says every
  government must provide free basic education for primary and JSS age, and parents must send
  children through both. Senior secondary is not covered.
- **School year:** three terms (roughly September–December, January–April, April/May–July).
- **Changing now: 12-year basic education.** Proposed in February 2025 (one continuous 12 years,
  no exam barrier between JSS 3 and SS 1). July 2026 reports say the government has started
  phasing out the JSS/SSS split and sent a bill to the National Assembly; the ministry also says
  the 6-3-3 classes stay. **Design for the class names (JSS 1, SS 1…) to stay, and for the BECE
  to possibly stop being a gate.**
- **Language of instruction:** English at every level. The 2022 mother-tongue policy (local
  language up to Primary 6) was cancelled in November 2025. A Nigerian language is still a
  subject. (Many early learners and parents are more fluent in Hausa, Yoruba, Igbo or Pidgin:
  a help, not the medium.)

## 2. Curriculum (2025 review, from the 2025/2026 session)

Reviewed by NERDC with UBEC, NSSEC and NBTE: fewer subjects, trade subjects added, digital skills
throughout. Subject names as published (Premium Times, from the ministry's release):

- **Primary 1–3 (9–10 subjects):** English Studies, Mathematics, One Nigerian Language, Basic
  Science, Physical and Health Education, Christian Religious Studies / Islamic Studies, Nigerian
  History, Social and Citizenship Studies, Cultural and Creative Arts, Arabic (optional).
- **Primary 4–6:** the above, with Basic Science and Technology instead of Basic Science, plus
  Basic Digital Literacy, Pre-vocational Studies, French (optional).
- **JSS 1–3 (12–14 subjects):** English Studies, Mathematics, One Nigerian Language, Intermediate
  Science, Physical and Health Education, Digital Technologies, CRS / IS, Nigerian History, Social
  and Citizenship Studies, Cultural and Creative Arts, Business Studies, French / Arabic
  (optional), and **one trade subject**.
- **SS 1–3 (8–9 subjects):** five compulsory (English Language, General Mathematics, Citizenship
  and Heritage Studies, Digital Technologies, one trade subject) plus a track:
  - Sciences: Biology, Chemistry, Physics, Agriculture, Further Mathematics, Physical Education,
    Health Education, Foods & Nutrition, Geography, Technical Drawing.
  - Humanities: Nigerian History, Government, CRS, IS, a Nigerian language, French, Arabic,
    Visual Arts, Music, Literature in English, Home Management, Catering Craft.
  - Business: Accounting, Commerce, Marketing, Economics.
- **Trade subjects:** Solar PV installation and maintenance; Fashion design and garment making;
  Livestock farming; Beauty and cosmetology; Computer hardware and GSM repairs; Horticulture and
  crop production.
- Early years follow NERDC's ECCDE curriculum (early literacy, numeracy, science and the world
  around the child, creative arts, social habits), mostly through play.

Caveats: sources disagree on the P4–6 subject count (10–12 vs 11–13), the senior secondary
review was still being finished when this was announced, and rollout is uneven. Many schools
still teach to the old subject names, and the exams lag the curriculum. **Topic lists per class
and term (schemes of work) must come from NERDC documents, not from memory.**

## 3. External examinations

| Exam | Body | Who sits it | When | Format / notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **NCEE** (Common Entrance) | NECO | Primary 6, for JSS 1 in Federal Unity Colleges | Once a year (e.g. June) | ~57,000 candidates in 2026. States run their own entrance exams for state schools. |
| **BECE** (Junior WAEC) | NECO for federal schools; state ministries for state schools | JSS 3 | April–May | 12 subjects for NECO's 2026 sitting (~186,000 candidates); resits in Mathematics and English. May stop being a gate under 12-year basic education. |
| **WASSCE (school)** | WAEC | SS 3 | April–June | Grades A1, B2, B3, C4, C5, C6 (credits), D7, E8 (passes), F9 (fail). Mostly pen and paper; computer-based (CBT) in a small share of schools so far. |
| **WASSCE (private / "GCE")** | WAEC | Anyone out of school | Two series (Jan–Feb, Oct–Nov) | **Computer-based since 2024.** ~10,500 candidates in the first 2026 series, ~56,000 in the second. |
| **NECO SSCE (internal)** | NECO | SS 3 | June–July | Same grade scale; accepted like WASSCE. |
| **NECO SSCE external (GCE)** | NECO | Anyone out of school | Nov–Dec | Hybrid CBT and paper. ₦30,000 for 2026 (more for late or walk-in registration); needs a NIN. |
| **NBC / NTC** | NABTEB | Technical and business students, trades | May–June and Nov–Dec | Accepted as O'level equivalents for many courses; also modular trade certificates. Fits the new trade subjects. |
| **UTME** | JAMB | Anyone seeking university, polytechnic or college of education | April | All CBT. Use of English plus three subjects set by the course. Minimum admission age 16 (by 30 September), with a narrow exception for gifted candidates. Then each institution's **Post-UTME** screening. |

- **The O'level rule that matters most:** most universities need **five credits (A1–C6) including
  English and Mathematics**, from no more than two sittings (rules vary by institution). Many
  candidates combine WAEC and NECO results to get there.
- **Direction of travel:** every major exam is moving to computer-based testing. JAMB already is,
  WAEC private candidates already are, and NECO external is partly. **Learners who have never used
  a computer exam are at a disadvantage, and practice in the same format is valuable.**
- **Copyright:** past questions belong to WAEC, NECO and JAMB. Dopple must write its own practice
  questions to the published syllabuses (or license past papers), not copy them.

## 4. Who is left out, and why

- **About 18.3 million children out of school** (UNICEF 2024: 10.2 million of primary age, 8.1
  million of secondary age). The north-west carries the most: Kano, Katsina and Jigawa alone
  account for about 16% of the primary-age total.
- **Learning poverty:** 73% of 7–14-year-olds cannot read a simple sentence and 75% cannot do
  simple maths (UNICEF, via BusinessDay). Many children *in* school need catch-up too.
- **Poverty:** 47% of children live below the national poverty line. Even "free" public school
  costs money for uniforms, books, levies and transport, and older children are kept home to
  work.
- **Groups hardest hit:** almajiri pupils (officially counted as out of school), nomadic
  communities, children with disabilities, displaced children (insecurity in the north-east and
  north-west), and girls in some regions.
- **Dropouts at transitions:** most primary-age out-of-school children never enrolled; most
  secondary-age ones dropped out. The JSS 3 → SS 1 step is a known exit point.
- **Second-chance learners:** young adults retaking WASSCE/NECO as private candidates (tens of
  thousands each series), people who left school and want an O'level, and adults learning to read
  (the 2014 policy promises them functional basic education).

## 5. What limits learning online

- **Access is real but thin.** Internet penetration is about 50% (NCC, late 2025) with roughly 145
  million internet subscriptions, but smartphones reach 59% of urban and only 26% of rural people
  (GSMA, 2023). Phones are often shared, so a child may get the parent's phone in the evening.
- **Data is expensive.** Tariffs rose 50% in January 2025, and 1 GB now costs about ₦500–800
  (TechCabal's operator survey). A video-first course can cost a family more than a day's food.
- **Power is unreliable.** Phones die and charging costs money, so sessions are short.
- **Low-end Android, small screens, older browsers,** and storage that is already full.
- **Exams demand English** while many early learners think in another language.
- **Children's data is protected.** Under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 a child is under
  18, and processing their data needs a parent's or guardian's consent (with an education
  exception to check with a lawyer).

## 6. What already exists

- **Nigeria Learning Passport** (UNICEF and the federal government): free, curriculum-aligned,
  pre-primary to secondary, online and offline (school hubs), with data paid by Airtel. About 2.4
  million users across 25 states. Linked to the government's **Open School Programme** for
  dropouts. Dopple should complement it and point to it, not compete.
- Commercial apps (exam prep, video lessons) mostly target paying families in cities and lean on
  video.

## 7. What this means for Dopple ("Dopple Learn")

1. **Free, with no account needed to start learning.** Anyone with the link can learn. Progress
   stays on the device until someone chooses to make an account (which, for a child, needs a
   parent).
2. **Text first and tiny.** Every lesson works on 2G, as a few KB of text, with images only where
   they teach. No video by default. Pages load fast on a ₦50,000 phone, and later work offline.
3. **Organised the way school is:** stage → class → subject → topic, using the 2025 curriculum's
   subject names, so a child can keep pace with their age group or catch up on a missed class.
4. **Practice the way exams work:** short objective questions (A–D) with an explanation for every
   answer, and later timed, CBT-style mock papers for NCEE, BECE, WASSCE/NECO (school and
   private) and UTME, written by Dopple to the syllabus.
5. **Exam guidance for people out of school:** which exam to sit, the five-credit rule, how to
   register as a private candidate, and what UTME needs for a course.
6. **Built for second chances:** placement ("where should I start?"), short sessions, and adults
   and young people as well as children.
7. **Schools pay, learners don't:** paying schools fund the free side; the same lessons help their
   students revise. Data partnerships (zero-rating, like Airtel with the Learning Passport) are
   worth pursuing.
8. **Content quality is the product.** Lessons and questions are written or checked by qualified
   teachers, versioned in the repo, and reviewed in pull requests.
